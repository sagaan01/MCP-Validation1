Time value of money

Why ₹1 (or $1) today beats the same amount later — and the formulas that make wealth math click in interviews.

Layman idea

Money has a time price. If someone offers you cash now or the same cash next year, take it now — because today’s money can earn a return (or buy something you need today). That opportunity cost is the heart of finance. CFA framing calls the interest rate a required return, a discount rate, or an opportunity cost — three names for the same idea.

Small story

Maya can take a client’s $1,000 fee today, or wait one year. Her savings earn 5%. If she waits, she still gets $1,000 — but she misses the $50 her money could have earned. So the “same” $1,000 next year is weaker. That’s time value of money (TVM).

Compounding (interest on interest)

Per SEC Investor.gov: start with $100 at 5%. Year 1 → $105. Year 2 → $110.25 — you earned $5 on the original $100 and $0.25 on the first year’s interest. Leave it alone 10 years → more than $162; 25 years → almost $340. No extra deposits. Time did the work.

FV = PV × (1 + r)n

Example: PV = 100, r = 0.05, n = 2 → FV = 100 × 1.05² = 110.25.

PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n

Flip it: what is $110.25 in two years worth today at 5%? → $100.

Rule of 72 (life formula)

Years to double ≈ 72 ÷ (annual rate %)

SEC example: at ~9%, 72 ÷ 9 ≈ 8 years to double. Interview line: “I use the Rule of 72 to sanity-check compounding speed.”
